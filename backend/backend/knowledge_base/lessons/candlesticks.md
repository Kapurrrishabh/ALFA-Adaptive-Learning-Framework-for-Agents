# Candlestick patterns

## Candle anatomy
A candle has a body (open to close) and wicks or shadows (to the high and low).
A close above the open is a bullish (white or green) candle; below, bearish. The
ratio of body to full range tells whether buyers or sellers controlled the
session; long wicks show prices that were rejected.

## Doji
The open and close are almost equal (body at most about 10% of the range). A doji
signals indecision. After a long trend it can precede a pause or reversal, but on
its own it has no direction.

## Hammer and hanging man
Both have a small body near the top of the range and a lower shadow at least
twice the body. After a decline it is a hammer (possible bullish reversal); after
an advance the same shape is a hanging man (possible bearish warning).

## Inverted hammer and shooting star
Both have a small body near the bottom of the range and a long upper shadow.
After a decline it is an inverted hammer; after an advance it is a shooting star,
which shows buyers pushed prices up and then lost control.

## Engulfing patterns
A bullish engulfing pattern is a bearish candle followed by a larger bullish
candle whose body covers the prior body, after a decline. A bearish engulfing
pattern is the mirror image after an advance.

## Harami
A harami is a large candle followed by a small candle whose body sits inside the
prior body. It signals that the prior move has lost momentum.

## Morning star and evening star
A morning star is a three-candle bullish reversal: a large bearish candle, a
small-bodied candle, then a bullish candle closing above the midpoint of the
first. An evening star is the bearish mirror image at the end of an advance.

## Piercing line and dark cloud cover
A piercing line opens below the prior bearish candle's close and closes above its
midpoint. Dark cloud cover opens above the prior bullish candle's close and closes
below its midpoint.

## Three white soldiers and three black crows
Three consecutive strong candles in the same direction, each opening inside the
prior body and closing near its extreme. They show sustained pressure in one
direction.

## What the evidence says about candlesticks
Academic tests are mostly unfavourable once realistic costs are included. For
example, Marshall, Young and Rose (2006, Journal of Banking & Finance) found
candlestick strategies did not add value for Dow Jones Industrial Average stocks.
Results vary by market and period. This is why the system never treats a
formation as a signal on its own: it checks prior trend, volume, location near
support or resistance, and subsequent confirmation, and it measures whether the
formation has had any edge on the specific stock's own history before giving it
weight.
