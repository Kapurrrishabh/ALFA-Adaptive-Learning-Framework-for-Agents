# Mutual funds and ETFs

## Mutual fund
A mutual fund pools money from many investors. A professional fund manager
invests it in a portfolio of stocks, bonds or both. Each investor owns units of
the fund. The value of a unit moves with the value of what the fund holds. In
India, mutual funds are regulated by SEBI and run by asset management companies
(AMCs). A fund spreads your money across many holdings. So if one company does
badly, it hurts less than if you had bought only that company.

## NAV (net asset value)
The NAV is the value of one unit of a mutual fund. It is the market value of
everything the fund holds, minus its costs, divided by the number of units.
Indian funds publish their NAV once a day, after markets close. You buy or sell
units at that day's NAV. A high or low NAV says nothing about whether a fund is
cheap or expensive. Only the change in NAV over time tells you how it has done.

## SIP (systematic investment plan)
A SIP invests a fixed amount in a mutual fund at a regular interval, usually
each month, by automatic debit. Since the amount is fixed, it buys more units
when the NAV is low and fewer when it is high. This averages the cost per unit
over time (rupee cost averaging). A SIP builds the habit of investing regularly.
You need not pick a moment to invest. But a SIP does not guarantee a profit or
protect against losses in a falling market.

## Lump sum versus SIP
A lump sum invests the whole amount at once. A SIP spreads it over months. In a
market that rises over the period, a lump sum usually ends ahead, because all of
the money was invested for longer. In one that falls first, a SIP usually does
better. Nobody knows which will happen. So a SIP mainly reduces the regret of
investing everything just before a fall.

## Expense ratio
The expense ratio is the yearly fee a fund charges, as a percentage of the money
it manages. It is taken out of the fund's assets each day. So the NAV you see is
already after the fee. A difference of even one percentage point a year
compounds into a large gap over decades. That is why low-cost index funds often
beat higher-cost active funds after fees. SEBI sets maximum expense ratios by
fund size.

## Direct and regular plans
Every Indian mutual fund scheme comes in two versions. A direct plan is bought
straight from the fund house, or a platform that offers direct plans. A regular
plan is bought through a distributor, who is paid a commission out of the fund's
expenses. Both hold the same portfolio. But the regular plan has a higher
expense ratio, so its NAV grows more slowly.

## Index funds and ETFs
An index fund copies an index, such as the NIFTY 50, instead of having a manager
choose stocks. So it usually costs less, and its result is close to the index's,
minus fees and a small tracking error (the gap from the index). An ETF
(exchange-traded fund) does the same, but is bought and sold on the stock
exchange during market hours, like a share. So you need a demat account (which
holds shares) and pay brokerage. An index fund is bought from the fund house at
the end-of-day NAV.

## ELSS (tax-saving funds, verify current rules)
An Equity Linked Savings Scheme is an equity mutual fund. Its investments have
qualified for a tax deduction under section 80C of the old income tax regime.
Each investment is locked in for three years. Whether the deduction helps
depends on which tax regime you choose. Check the current rules before relying
on it.

## Exit load
An exit load is a fee some funds charge if you sell units within a set period after
buying them. For equity funds, it is often a percentage of the amount sold
within the first year. It discourages short-term trading in a fund meant for
longer holding. The scheme's documents state the exact load and period.

## Equity, debt and hybrid funds
Equity funds invest mainly in shares and move the most in value. Debt funds
invest in bonds and money-market instruments and move less. But they carry
interest-rate and credit risk. Hybrid funds hold both. SEBI groups equity funds
into categories such as large cap, mid cap, small cap, flexi cap and multi cap,
defined by the share of the portfolio in companies of each size. So funds in the
same category can be compared.
