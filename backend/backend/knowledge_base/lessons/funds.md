# Mutual funds and ETFs

## Mutual fund
A mutual fund pools money from many investors and invests it in a portfolio of
stocks, bonds or both, run by a professional fund manager. Each investor owns units
of the fund, and the value of a unit moves with the value of what the fund holds.
In India mutual funds are regulated by SEBI and run by asset management companies
(AMCs). A fund spreads your money across many holdings, so one company doing badly
hurts less than if you had bought only that company.

## NAV (net asset value)
The NAV is the value of one unit of a mutual fund: the market value of everything
the fund holds, minus its costs, divided by the number of units. Indian funds
publish their NAV once a day, after markets close, and you buy or sell units at
that day's NAV. A high or low NAV says nothing about whether a fund is cheap or
expensive; only the change in NAV over time tells you how it has done.

## SIP (systematic investment plan)
A SIP invests a fixed amount in a mutual fund at a regular interval, usually every
month, by automatic debit. Because the amount is fixed, it buys more units when the
NAV is low and fewer when it is high, which averages the cost per unit over time
(rupee cost averaging). A SIP builds the habit of investing regularly and removes
the need to pick a moment to invest; it does not guarantee a profit or protect
against losses in a falling market.

## Lump sum versus SIP
A lump sum invests the whole amount at once; a SIP spreads it over months. In a
market that rises over the period a lump sum usually ends ahead, because all of the
money was invested for longer; in one that falls first, a SIP usually does better.
Since nobody knows which will happen, a SIP mainly reduces the regret of investing
everything just before a fall.

## Expense ratio
The expense ratio is the yearly fee a fund charges, as a percentage of the money
it manages. It is taken out of the fund's assets every day, so the NAV you see is
already after the fee. A difference of even one percentage point a year compounds
into a large gap over decades, which is why low-cost index funds often beat
higher-cost active funds after fees. SEBI sets maximum expense ratios by fund size.

## Direct and regular plans
Every Indian mutual fund scheme comes in a direct plan, bought straight from the
fund house or a platform that offers direct plans, and a regular plan, bought
through a distributor who is paid a commission out of the fund's expenses. The two
hold the same portfolio; the regular plan has a higher expense ratio, so its NAV
grows more slowly.

## Index funds and ETFs
An index fund copies an index such as the NIFTY 50 instead of having a manager
choose stocks, so it usually costs less and its result is close to the index's,
minus fees and a small tracking error. An ETF (exchange-traded fund) does the same
but is bought and sold on the stock exchange during market hours like a share, so
you need a demat account and pay brokerage; an index fund is bought from the fund
house at the end-of-day NAV.

## ELSS (tax-saving funds, verify current rules)
An Equity Linked Savings Scheme is an equity mutual fund whose investments have
qualified for a tax deduction under section 80C of the old income tax regime, with
a lock-in of three years from each investment. Whether the deduction helps you
depends on which tax regime you choose; check the current rules before relying on
it.

## Exit load
An exit load is a fee some funds charge if you sell units within a set period after
buying them, often a percentage of the amount sold within the first year for
equity funds. It discourages short-term trading in a fund meant for longer holding.
The scheme's documents state the exact load and period.

## Equity, debt and hybrid funds
Equity funds invest mainly in shares and swing the most in value. Debt funds invest
in bonds and money-market instruments and move less, though they carry interest-rate
and credit risk. Hybrid funds hold both. SEBI groups equity funds into categories
such as large cap, mid cap, small cap, flexi cap and multi cap, defined by the share
of the portfolio in companies of each size, so funds within a category can be
compared with each other.
